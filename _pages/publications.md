## Working Papers

### Output without Workers: Automation and Structural Transformation

2026
{: .entry-meta}

Buera and Kaboski (2009) leave an open question in the structural transformation literature: why do sectoral value-added shares and employment shares so often diverge? This paper answers that question by bringing automation's displacement effect into the structural transformation literature and quantifying it. In a task-based model with non-homothetic CES demand, value-added shares are generated by the price and income effects already familiar from that literature, while employment shares require a third force beyond them: a labor-saving displacement effect that reassigns tasks from workers to capital without moving value added at first order. Modifying the growth accounting procedure of Jones and Tonetti (2026), I quantify this displacement effect from standard national-accounts data alone; an assumption on task-level labor productivity is required only to interpret the recovered term as automation's displacement. In the US, displacement accounts for 64% of the model-predicted agriculture–manufacturing and 35% of the services–manufacturing employment reallocation over 1951–1989, rising to 188% and 73% over 1990–2019. After 1990, labor-saving task reallocation—with automation as its leading source—shifts from agriculture to manufacturing.
